# RVS — Go To Market

**Version:** 1.1 · September 6, 2026
**Scope:** Who to approach, in what order, and what to say. Positioning is in `RVS_Positioning.md`; objections in `RVS_Objections.md`.

---

## The motion

Design partners first, revenue second — but **priced from the first conversation.** The product is a form and an email: it demos in ninety seconds, needs no integration, no IT involvement and no procurement, and it stands up in one field while the prospect watches. That is the whole advantage. Spend it on getting into shops, not on building collateral.

Pricing is settled (Q6) and lives in `RVS_Positioning.md`. Quote it. The discount is time, never the number.

Two prospects are already live and they are the plan. Everything below is about widening that to a handful of design partners, not about a funnel.

---

## Ideal early customer

Ranked by how fast they can say yes.

| Segment | Why | Decision path |
|---|---|---|
| **Mobile RV technicians** | Intake chaos is their daily pain; a wasted trip has a dollar figure attached. No DMS, no IT, no committee | Owner decides on the spot |
| **Independent repair shops** | Usually no structured intake at all — phone, paper, scattered photos | Owner, yes or no |
| **Collision / insurance shops** | Documentation-heavy work; photos before arrival materially help estimates | Owner or manager |
| **Regional dealer groups** | Real volume, but their location pages capture nothing. Ask a service manager, never a CIO | Service manager for a pilot; procurement if you get routed upward |

Avoid national chains for now. Procurement friction and IT review will cost months and teach you less than one afternoon in a shop.

## The two live prospects

**Mobile technician.** Lead with wasted trips. He drives out, the failure is a different component than the phone call suggested, and he eats the trip. Structured intake with photos and a decoded VIN before he leaves means the right part is on the truck. He never logs into anything — the write-up hits his email.

**Decided — Q3 closed: a subscription, never equity.** Free for six months, then $39/mo, locked at that rate for life as a founding-partner price. Equity is refused for four reasons, and they are worth being able to say out loud: it trades a percentage of the company for advice worth a few thousand dollars; it converts a customer into an owner, which destroys the value as evidence — *"a shop that pays"* is a proof point, *"my partner likes it"* is not; it puts a name on the cap table before there is anything to raise against; and it cannot be undone. If he wants upside, the answer is a named referral fee — a revenue share or free service for each shop he brings — which costs nothing until it works. An advisory title, if he wants one, is free.

**Dealer group.** Lead with their own web form. Their location pages collect a name and a callback request; replace that with structured triage arriving in the service inbox as a one-page write-up. No new logins, no DMS project. Ask to turn it on at one store — two if they offer.

Who owns the location service pages — marketing, IT, or an outside agency — is Q4, and it is worth knowing. It is **not** a gate on pitching, because the pilot can be built to route around it entirely.

**The pilot must require nothing the service manager cannot authorize alone.** If the web pages belong to an agency, do not ask for a website change at all. Ask for three things inside his own control:

- a QR code at the service counter
- the intake link in his advisors' email signatures
- the intake link in the service department's existing "we'll call you back" autoresponder

The web form replacement becomes the *expansion* ask, made after packets are landing and he has a reason of his own to push on marketing. That turns Q4 from a blocker into a second-conversation topic.

## Local design partners

The founder is in Washington, Utah, within ~120 miles of a usable cluster of shops. That proximity allows something most founders cannot do: **watch the service desk in person.** How advisors answer the phone, how jobs reach techs, how repair notes actually get written. Those observations are worth more than any survey.

| Candidate | Type | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Lazydays RV, Washington UT (formerly RVzz) | Independent service shop, since acquired | Closest. See the caution below |
| RV Doctor Mobile Service, St. George | Mobile owner-operator | Closest analogue to the live mobile-tech prospect |
| Adventure RV Service Center, Hurricane | Independent service shop | Service-heavy, independent ownership, fast decisions |
| Motor Sportsland RV Service, St. George | Small regional dealer group | One slightly larger service department without national-chain bureaucracy |
| Cedar City RV & Trailer Repair | Independent shop | Geographic diversity, tests remote onboarding |

**Caution on Lazydays.** Earlier notes named RVzz the single best first partner. It has since been acquired by Lazydays, and the field research in `Dealerships/LazyDays_Repair.md` documents sustained customer complaints about warranty handling and — directly relevant — about communication: no updates unless the customer initiates, service managers not returning calls, week-long response gaps.

That cuts both ways. It is exactly the problem RVS addresses, which makes it a sharp pitch. But post-acquisition it is no longer a fast independent decision, and a shop with a communication problem may have a management problem rather than a tooling problem. Treat it as a prospect, not as the anchor design partner.

## Outreach

Cold email and LinkedIn work in this niche — it is small, and operators are used to vendors. Keep it to four lines.

**Attach a real packet.** This is the highest-leverage thing in the whole motion and it costs one PDF. The marketing asset and the product's output are the same object — a rare property, and it should be spent. Do not describe the packet in a sentence when you can send one.

> Subject: Reducing service phone calls at RV dealerships
>
> Hi [Name],
>
> Attached is what lands in a service inbox about sixty seconds after an RV owner fills out a form on their phone — VIN decoded, photos, their own description, and answers to three follow-up questions. It prints, and the bottom block pastes into a DMS complaint field.
>
> I'm looking for a couple of shops willing to try it and tell me what's wrong with it. Ten minutes?

That does the ninety-second demo's work before you are in the room, and it filters for the people who care about the artifact.

Ask a service manager. Not a CIO, not an owner if there is a service manager. The service manager feels the problem hourly.

## The demo

Ninety seconds, on a phone, live. Do not present slides.

1. **Fill out the form as a customer would.** VIN, description, two photos, answer the AI follow-up questions.
2. **Open the inbox.** Show the email that just arrived — the write-up, the photos, the printable PDF.
3. **Show the paste block.** Point at it and say: *"That drops into the complaint field in your DMS without retyping anything."*

Then stop talking. The interesting question is whether they reach for the PDF or the paste block — that tells you which half of the product they actually want.

Do not demo the manager app. A manager should be able to run a full week on email alone; showing a dashboard undercuts the one claim that differentiates the product.

### The close is a configuration, not a signature

Setup is one field — the recipient address. That fact is currently filed as an objection handler in `RVS_Objections.md`, which wastes it. It is a closing tool.

Ask for the service inbox address in the room and type it in while they watch. Do not leave with *"we'll get back to you"*; leave with a live location slug and their inbox configured. If a location takes more than ten minutes to stand up in front of a prospect, that is a product bug worth fixing before the next pitch.

And stop saying **pilot**. "Pilot" summons an evaluation committee; a pilot is a project. Say *"let's turn it on for one store."* Turning something on is a setting.

## Terms, and the first sixty days

**Sixty days free, at a named price, with a stated end date.**

> "It's $79 a month per store. The first sixty days are free — if it isn't worth $79 by then, I want to know why, and I'll turn it off."

Open-ended free was the plan and it was wrong. Free with no end date produces polite non-adoption and no signal at all: a yes costs the buyer nothing, so it tells you nothing, and a free thing has no value anchor to renew against. A named price gets a real answer either way.

Naming the number also makes the pitch stronger, not weaker. Refusing to quote reads as hiding something or as not knowing your own business, and with a service manager the second is worse. The rule is now **quote the price, discount the time** — prices are in `RVS_Positioning.md`, and they are the same number for everyone.

### The one-page agreement gates go-live

The terms a design partner signs must grant a perpetual, irrevocable license to use their service data in anonymized, aggregate form (Spec X-3). It costs nothing now and cannot be retrofitted later without renegotiating with every existing customer from a weaker position. Never pitch it. It is a clause, not a feature.

But closing by live configuration means packets can start flowing before anything is signed, so: **the agreement is one page, e-signable from a phone, and signed before the slug goes live.** A three-page PDF that needs a printer will lose the race against the enthusiasm you just generated in the room.

Treat drafting it as a blocking item alongside build items 1–2, not as paperwork to sort out later. It is a product dependency, not a legal chore.

## What proves it worked

Borrowed from `../RVS_Plan.md`, because these are sales claims as much as engineering ones:

- The paste block goes into a real DMS complaint field without reformatting — **verified against an actual DMS, not a mock.**
- The PDF prints legibly on a shop printer, in greyscale.
- A service manager runs a full week on email alone and never opens the manager app.

That last one is the real test, and it is also the best case study sentence available: *"they never logged in."*

### Collect one number from the first packet onward

The ship criteria above are engineering claims. Add one sales claim that is free to measure and brutal against a dealer's status quo: **the share of requests arriving with a decoded VIN, photos, and answered diagnostic questions.** Their current form captures a name and a callback request, so the comparison is against zero.

*"Your form captures a name. All of these arrive with a VIN, photos, and answers to three diagnostic questions"* is a stronger sentence than any time-saved estimate, and unlike a case study, it is available on day one.

## Deliberately not doing yet

No website, no content marketing, no conference presence, no case studies, no ARR target driving behaviour. At two prospects and zero shipped packets, all of that is premature. Revisit when three shops are running on it.

**One exception, because it is free: the mobile technician's own customers.** A QR code on the truck and on his invoices tests the customer side of the funnel — do RV owners actually finish the form? — at zero cost, and it is the one question no amount of shop-side research answers. It is also the cheapest source of the demand signal that makes dealer outreach warm, which is a shop hearing *"can I use that form?"* from three of its own customers.
